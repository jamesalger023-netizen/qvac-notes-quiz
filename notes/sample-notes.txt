Photosynthesis — Biology 101 notes

Photosynthesis is the process plants, algae, and some bacteria use to convert
light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. It happens mainly in the
chloroplasts of plant cells, in structures called thylakoids, using the
pigment chlorophyll to absorb light (mostly red and blue wavelengths; green
is reflected, which is why plants look green).

The overall reaction is:
6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy -> C6H12O6 + 6 O2

Photosynthesis has two main stages:

1. Light-dependent reactions (in the thylakoid membrane)
   - Sunlight is absorbed by chlorophyll.
   - Water molecules are split (photolysis), releasing oxygen as a byproduct.
   - Energy is captured in ATP and NADPH, which are used in the next stage.

2. Light-independent reactions / Calvin cycle (in the stroma)
   - Does not require light directly, but depends on the ATP and NADPH made
     in stage 1.
   - Carbon dioxide from the air is "fixed" into organic molecules.
   - Over multiple turns of the cycle, glucose is built up.

Factors that affect the rate of photosynthesis:
- Light intensity: more light generally increases the rate, up to a point.
- CO2 concentration: more available CO2 can increase the rate.
- Temperature: enzymes involved have an optimal temperature range; too hot or
  too cold slows the reaction.

Photosynthesis is the foundation of most food chains on Earth and is the
main source of atmospheric oxygen.
